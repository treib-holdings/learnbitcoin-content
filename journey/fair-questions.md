**A guess first.** In 2021 China banned bitcoin mining, the work of making Bitcoin's blocks. More of that work was done in China than in any other country. How long did Bitcoin stop for: a day, a month, or not at all?

## Is it a scam?

Bitcoin itself is a network, not a company collecting your money or promising you a return. But scammers use Bitcoin, just as they use other forms of money. Once a Bitcoin transaction is confirmed, you generally can't reverse it if you sent money to the wrong person.

Watch out for a few common tricks:

- **The "too good to be true" investment.** Someone promises you a guaranteed profit if you send them bitcoin. They may show you a website with impressive gains, then demand more money before you can withdraw.
- **The urgent call.** Someone pretending to be your bank, the government, or a company says your money is in danger and tells you to send bitcoin or use a Bitcoin ATM. Hang up and contact the organization using a number you find yourself.
- **The request for your recovery words.** Someone posing as a wallet company or offering to help asks for your 12 or 24 words. Those words give them control of your wallet. Never share them.

If someone pressures you to act quickly, stop. Don't send money or share your recovery words. Talk it through with someone you trust.

## Why is it worth anything, and why does the price fall?

Bitcoin has value because people want it. Some use it to send money without a bank's approval. Others value being able to hold it themselves or prefer its predictable supply. Some buy it hoping its price will rise.

Bitcoin's rules limit its supply to roughly 21 million coins. New bitcoin is still being created, but at a decreasing rate. No single person can change the limit for everyone.

Scarcity alone doesn't guarantee value, though. People also have to want what's scarce. Critics question whether Bitcoin's uses justify its price; supporters believe those uses and its limited supply give it lasting value.

Bitcoin's price changes as buyers and sellers decide what they're willing to pay. News, expectations and speculation can move it sharply. Since 2011, it has fallen by more than half seven times. It has recovered from previous falls, but there's no guarantee it will recover from the next one.

Unlike a savings account, bitcoin pays no interest on its own. And the amount of dollars you can exchange it for can change dramatically, even over a short time.

## Who really runs it?

Bitcoin has no owner or central operator. Two groups do different jobs to keep it running.

**Miners** compete to add blocks of transactions to the record. The miner that adds a valid block receives newly created bitcoin, along with transaction fees. Mining uses a great deal of electricity.

**Nodes** independently check transactions and blocks against Bitcoin's rules. Anyone can run one, and thousands of people and organizations do.

If a miner creates a block that breaks the rules, full nodes reject it, no matter how large the miner is. Miners cannot simply create extra bitcoin or spend yours without your keys.

But mining power matters too. A few large mining pools produce much of Bitcoin's blocks. If enough mining power acted together, it could delay transactions or reorganize recent blocks to reverse its own payments. It could not make full nodes accept coins created outside their rules.

A dispute in 2017 showed the limits of miners' influence. Many major companies and miners supported a proposal, which included increasing the block size limit. But the proposed increase faced significant opposition, and its organizers called it off, saying they had not built enough consensus.

Bitcoin's rules can change, and other changes did go through around that time. But being big, or controlling a lot of mining power, does not give anyone the power to change the rules for everyone.

## Could a government shut it down?

None has, and there is no switch to turn off. The nodes and miners are spread over many countries.

The hardest test came in 2021, when China banned mining. For some weeks blocks came more slowly, in the worst week about every seventeen minutes instead of ten. They never stopped. Within eight months more mining was going on than before the ban.

What a government can control is the companies around Bitcoin. Most people buy and sell through an **exchange**: a company that trades bitcoin for ordinary money, and usually holds its customers' coins. A government can license an exchange, order it to freeze a customer's account, or close it.

It can also take coins when it gets hold of the keys, because whoever has the keys can move the coins. In 2022 US agents found the keys to more than 94,000 stolen bitcoin in an online account, and took the coins.

## Is it legal? Is it taxed? Is it private?

In the United States it is legal to own, buy and sell. There, buying it and holding it is not taxed. Selling it, or paying for something with it, has to be reported, and a profit can be taxed. Rules differ by country: a few ban it outright, and more bar their banks from handling it. Your own tax office's website is the place to look.

It is less private than people think. Every payment stays on a public record forever. The record shows addresses, not names, but investigators have followed it to arrests and to recovered money.

Criminals do use it, as they use every kind of money, and ransom demands often ask for it. The US Treasury said in 2026 that they move much less through digital coins than through ordinary money, and that they are using the coins more.

## Should I buy some?

This Journey does not say. Three things are true either way.

Nobody can promise you a profit. No government insures what you buy, the way US bank deposits are insured. And you can lose all of it: the price can fall, a company holding your coins can fail, or you can be tricked into sending them away. One government watchdog puts it plainly: be prepared to lose all the money you put in.

Most people own none. About one American adult in ten holds or uses any cryptocurrency, a Federal Reserve survey finds, and about one in fifty paid for something with it.

## A fair objection

"This is a site about Bitcoin. Of course it has an answer for everything."

It does not. Three questions have no settled answer. Nobody knows whether a fixed supply will hold its value over decades. Whether all that electricity is worth it depends on what you think Bitcoin is worth: supporters say the cost is what makes the record so hard to rewrite, and critics say other designs use a tiny fraction of it.

And the new coins that pay the miners are cut in half about every four years. Whether the fees can take their place is an open question, and researchers disagree.

For the rest, check us. More than half of the sources at the bottom of this page are governments and central banks, and some of them are Bitcoin's sharpest critics.

## Words you now know

- **Miner.** A person or company whose machines race to make the next block. The winner is paid in new coins and fees.
- **Node.** A computer that keeps a copy of the record and checks every block and payment against the rules.
- **Exchange.** A company where people buy and sell bitcoin for ordinary money. It usually holds its customers' coins.

If you stop here, you know what Bitcoin is, why it was made, and what the hard questions are. If you go on, nothing ahead needs money: the next chapter has you send a payment yourself, with practice coins.
