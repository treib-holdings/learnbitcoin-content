> **Where you're going:** This is the last chapter. It answers the questions people ask next, gives you six habits worth keeping whatever you do, and ends with something to do for the next person. You may also stop here.

In chapter 1 you watched your first practice coins arrive. In chapter 4 you sent some on to the next learner. You've been on both sides of a Bitcoin payment. That's enough experience to help someone else make their first one, even if you never bought any yourself.

**A guess first.** A friend texts, "I'm about to wipe my old phone. I installed the wallet app on the new one. It says zero. Where did my bitcoin go?" What would you ask them before they erase anything?

## What happened to your friend's wallet?

The bitcoin is still on the public record. Installing the app again can give your friend a perfectly good new wallet. Their old payments belong to addresses from the old wallet. That's why an empty balance on a new phone tells you so little.

Ask whether they restored the old wallet from its backup or tapped Create. Have them keep the old phone until they see a familiar receiving address and the expected transactions on the new one. They can do this privately. You can explain the difference without ever seeing a word from their paper.

Sometimes there's no paper. Then the old phone holds the only copy of the words. Most wallet apps can show them again, in the wallet's settings, and they go on paper, by hand, before anything is erased.

A phone that is lost or stolen needs the paper, and in a hurry. Bring the wallet back from the paper on another phone, then move the coins to a new wallet with new words. Without the paper, nothing brings the coins back.

## Pick the question that's yours

**"If I give someone an address, what can they see?"** They can look it up on the same public record where you found the newspaper headline in block zero. If five people pay the same address, each can look up all five payments. Give each person a fresh address from Receive. The wallet shows a new one once the last has been paid. That helps keep those payments separate, although later spending can still link them. [Bitcoin privacy](/rabbit-hole/bitcoin-privacy)

**"I'd keep a few dollars on my phone. What about my savings?"** A hardware wallet keeps the keys on a separate device. Your phone or computer can prepare a payment. You check the amount and address on the device's own screen before you approve it. You still need a backup if the device breaks. [Hardware wallets, by name](/rabbit-hole/wallets)

**"A coffee costs less than the fee on a busy day. How do people pay for one?"** Some shops accept Lightning. It lets people make small bitcoin payments without putting each payment in its own transaction on the public record. You need a wallet that supports it. Ask who controls the keys before you put money in. [Lightning](/glossary/lightning-network)

**"Who tells my wallet a payment has confirmed?"** A phone wallet usually asks someone else's computer about the record. You met the alternative in chapter 3. A full node checks blocks for itself. Connect a compatible wallet to your own node and you can check payments against your own copy of the record. [Run your own node](/rabbit-hole/run-your-own-node)

**"Could I lose one key and still recover my savings?"** A wallet can require any two of three keys to spend. That arrangement is called multisig. One missing key leaves you two. You also need a record of how the keys fit together, and the people who may one day inherit the wallet need to know how to use it. This is a topic to study before changing how you keep savings. [Multisig and inheritance](/rabbit-hole/multisig-and-inheritance)

## Six habits to keep

Most of the money that owners lose is not taken by hackers. The owner sends it, to someone who has deceived them. Of the money people told the FBI they lost in 2025 in cases involving bitcoin and coins like it, more than four fifths went that way. So most of these habits are about people.

Break-ins at exchanges are mostly not in the FBI's count. They can be very large, and they are the risk that comes with leaving coins at a company.

1. Write the seed words by hand. Keep copies in separate, secure places. Anyone who reads one copy can spend from the wallet. The words go into your own wallet when you restore it, and nowhere else. Nobody else ever needs them.
2. Reach a wallet maker or exchange at an address you know. Close unexpected messages that ask you to sign in, install an update or share your words.
3. Take a fresh receiving address from the wallet and check the whole destination before sending. For a large transfer, a small test first can catch a mistake. Check the large transfer too.
4. Walk away from guaranteed returns. Skip offers to unlock your balance by sending more money.
5. Before you erase or replace a device, make sure you can reach the right wallet on the replacement.
6. When somebody gives you a deadline, give yourself time. Anyone who demands payment in bitcoin is a scammer, whoever they say they are. If the request seems to come from someone you know, call them on a number you already have.

If you are ever robbed, report it. In the United States, type ic3.gov into your browser, and tell your local police. They don't charge. Anyone who offers to get your money back for a fee paid first is one more scammer.

## Do it once for the next person

Imagine the person who sent you this course has offered to send you a few sats. They write, "What do you need to give me, and how will you know it worked?"

Give them your answer in your own words. If you want to show them, open your practice wallet and find the address you would share for a practice payment. Then open a payment from an earlier lesson and show where its confirmations appear. You can do both parts on your own, with the practice coins you already used.

If you're helping someone new, let them hold their own phone. Ask them to show you where they'd receive a payment. When they find the address, they can show you that screen. Give them privacy whenever they handle their backup.

## A fair objection

"I understand how it works. I still prefer my bank."

What do you value about your bank? Perhaps someone can reset your password, help with a disputed charge, or answer the phone when a transfer goes wrong. Those are good reasons to use it. You might also know someone who has had a payment refused or an account frozen. The ability to pay someone directly can matter a great deal to them. You can understand both experiences and decide differently for yourself.

## Words you now know

- **Hardware wallet.** A small device that keeps your keys away from the internet.
- **Lightning.** A network that runs on top of Bitcoin, for small, fast payments.
- **Full node.** A node that keeps its own copy of the record and checks every block itself. You can run one at home.
- **Multisig.** A wallet that needs more than one key to spend.
