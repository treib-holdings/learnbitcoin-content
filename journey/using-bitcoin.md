> **Where you're going:** This chapter is optional. It gets you a wallet of your own for real bitcoin, and two careful ways to put a little into it. It names one wallet app and two companies. Nobody paid to be named.

In the last chapter, you brought a wallet back from a piece of paper. You can do the same thing with real bitcoin. One thing has to change first. The wallet you practiced with, and the words you wrote down for it, belong to the practice network. Leave them there.

**A guess first.** You tap Buy. A company takes your dollars and shows you a bitcoin balance. A minute later you drop your phone in the lake. Which gets you back to those coins: your account at the company, or the 12 words you wrote down?

## Start with an empty wallet

For a few dollars' worth of bitcoin, a wallet app on your phone will do. We recommend BlueWallet for this first payment. It works on iPhone and Android, you can use it without creating an account, and you hold the keys. We tested the steps for setting it up and recovering it from paper. Fake wallet apps exist, so don't search the app store for it. [Get the real app and follow the wallet guide.](/rabbit-hole/wallets)

Create a new Bitcoin wallet. Write its new words on paper, in order. While the wallet is still empty, follow the guide to restore it and check that the same receiving address comes back. Keep the paper private, the way you practiced in chapter 5.

Now you have a wallet that can receive real bitcoin. It can stay empty for as long as you like. The app shows a Buy Bitcoin button of its own. Leave it alone: it opens another seller's website.

## If someone sent you this course

Here's the small job we have for them. If they hold bitcoin and you'd like to try receiving some, ask whether they'd send you a few dollars' worth. Anyone else you know in person who holds bitcoin will do as well. If you're together, this takes two phones.

1. On yours, open your new wallet and tap Receive. The square code and the string of letters and numbers beneath it show the same address.
2. Have your friend scan the code, or send them the address in a message. An address isn't a secret. Your words are, and they never go in a message. Your friend compares the whole address on their payment screen with yours, enters a small amount, and reviews the network fee before they send. If the fee looks silly for the size of the gift, you can both come back another day.
3. Look on your phone. You may see the payment marked pending, which means it is waiting for a block. Open it again later. When it shows one confirmation, a miner has included it in a block. You saw this happen with practice coins in chapter 4. This time you could spend what arrived.

Your friend needed one address. They had no reason to see the paper or take your phone.

## If you'd like to buy a little yourself

An exchange lets you pay ordinary money for bitcoin. For a first purchase in the United States, we'd look at Strike (`strike.me`) or River (`river.com`). Both focus on bitcoin and let you send it to your own wallet. Nobody paid to be named here, and these are not links: type the company's web address yourself. An ad above the search results can lead to an imitation. If you want the company's app, take the link to the store from its own site.

Availability depends on where you live. River, for one, doesn't serve New York or Nevada. We read both companies' own pages on November 18, 2026. A named company can still fail, which is why this chapter ends with the coins in your own wallet. Somewhere else, or thinking of another company? [Check it first](#any-other-company-check-it-first).

Start from a web address you typed, never from one of these:

- **An app found by searching the app store, or the top result of a web search.** Fake apps get into the stores, and criminals buy advertisements that imitate a real company.
- **Someone who contacted you first,** or a link in a message.
- **An "investment" a friend invites you into.** A friend whose money is growing in an app may be in the middle of being fooled.
- **A Bitcoin ATM.** Some of these machines have kept more than a fifth of what people put in, and scammers send people to them.

**Already have an account at a big exchange?** You can use it for this. Stay out of the trading casino. You may see a list of "top gainers". A coin up 80% makes a good advertisement. That number tells you what happened to somebody who already owned it. It tells you nothing about what you'll earn by buying it now. Coins other than bitcoin are often called altcoins. Leave them alone for this exercise.

Skip any offer to borrow or trade with leverage. A button marked 5x lets $100 control about $500 of bitcoin. A 10% move against you costs about half of your $100, before fees, and the exchange can close the trade while the price is down. Even if bitcoin later recovers, your trade is over. You can learn everything in this chapter with one small purchase.

Before you put money in, answer three questions using the company's own fee and withdrawal information:

- Can I send bitcoin (BTC) over the Bitcoin network to my own address where I live?
- With the amount I'm comfortable spending, will I meet the withdrawal minimum? After the purchase and withdrawal costs, how much bitcoin will actually reach me?
- If I pay this way, how long before I can send it out? A company may let you buy immediately while your bank payment is still clearing.

If the minimum or the fees make a small purchase impractical, stop there. There's no reason to spend more to finish a lesson.

Expect two things, and neither is a bad sign. The company will ask for identification, as the law requires of it in most places. Fakes ask for it too, which is why you typed the web address yourself. And when you pay from a bank account, it may be some days before you can send out what you bought. At both companies named above that's usual. A wait is normal. A demand for more money is not.

If the numbers work for you, choose a one-time purchase of bitcoin (BTC) using money you already have. The exchange may call this a spot purchase. Spend only what you could afford to lose. Check the total before you agree. Once the purchase goes through, the company still holds the keys.

## Move it to your own wallet

Open BlueWallet and tap Receive in your real Bitcoin wallet. In the exchange, choose Send or Withdraw, select BTC, and choose the Bitcoin network for this address. Some companies call it on-chain. Copy or scan the address from BlueWallet. Compare the whole address on the final withdrawal screen with the one on your phone, then check the amount and fee. If anything differs, stop.

After you send, open BlueWallet and look for the payment. It may not show at once: some companies send withdrawals in batches, hours later, or a day or two later at the cheapest speed. Once you see the first confirmation, think back to the phone in the lake. Before withdrawal, you depended on your exchange account. Once the bitcoin arrives at an address from your own wallet, your paper backup can restore the keys that let you spend it.

If anyone says you must send them a separate payment to "unlock" a withdrawal, stop. People who pay do not get their money back. A real company takes its fee out of what you already hold there, and shows it in its own preview first. A demand to pay "tax" to an address someone sent you is a familiar scam. A scammer may even let you withdraw a little first to earn your trust.

## Any other company: check it first

The two names above are for the United States. Anywhere else, and for any other company, look for it first on a regulator's list. A **regulator** is a government office that licenses financial companies and keeps a list of them. New York's is short: the [list of companies licensed by New York State](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/apps_and_licensing/virtual_currency_businesses#regulated-entities), under the heading Regulated Entities. In the United Kingdom, use the [FCA Firm Checker](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/fca-firm-checker). Elsewhere, reach your regulator from your government's own website, not from a search.

Lists use legal names, so a brand may sit under a longer one: Strike is on New York's list as Zap Solutions, Inc. River is not on it, because it doesn't serve New York. Other US states keep their own lists. A look-alike doesn't count, and a fake can borrow a listed company's name, so type the web address yourself.

A license doesn't insure your coins, and licensed companies have failed. One stopped all withdrawals in July 2022, and in 2026 its customers were still waiting for the last of their money. That's one more reason to move the coins out. If a company is on no list you can find, choose another.

## Two things to do while you remember

**Keep the receipt.** Save the date, the amount of bitcoin, what you paid and the fees. For a gift, note when you received it and what it was worth. In the US, ask the giver to share when they acquired that bitcoin and what they paid for it. Those records can matter if you later sell or spend it: see the [US guidance on digital assets](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets). Rules differ by country, and your own tax office's website is the place to look.

Chapter 5 left a question about your backup. If something happened to you, would the person you want to inherit your bitcoin know it exists? Start by telling someone you trust that you have a wallet. Decide how they could find your instructions when needed. Keep the words themselves out of every message and email, and out of a will, which in many places becomes public. Anyone who finds them can spend the coins today. [Planning for inheritance](/glossary/bitcoin-inheritance-planning)
