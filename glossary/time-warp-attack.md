Bitcoin's [difficulty](/glossary/difficulty) is recalculated every 2,016 blocks from timestamps that miners write into the [block headers](/glossary/block-header) themselves. `GetNextWorkRequired` in `src/pow.cpp` finds the first block of the window and hands its timestamp to `CalculateNextWorkRequired`, which subtracts it from the last block's timestamp and compares the result with two weeks. Two details of that arithmetic matter here. The first and last block of a 2,016-block window are 2,015 intervals apart, not 2,016, so the comparison is off by one. And each window starts over with its own first block. Nothing ties the first timestamp of a window to the last timestamp of the window before it, except the [median time past](/glossary/mtp-median-time-past) rule, which only asks that a block be stamped later than the median of the previous eleven.

The off-by-one is harmless on its own. It spreads 1,209,600 seconds over 2,015 gaps instead of 2,016, so dividing two weeks by 2,015 gives ten minutes and three tenths of a second; once the randomness of block times is counted, blocks at constant hash rate average about ten minutes and six tenths. The seam between windows is the vulnerability. A miner who produces the last block of one window and the first block of the next may stamp the last with the true time and the first with a time far in the past, and both blocks are valid.

The attack built on that seam has been known since September 2011, when ArtForz described it on BitcoinTalk while it was being used against GeistGeld, a small experimental coin, and Gavin Andresen took his post to the bitcoin-dev list the next day. A miner with a majority of the [hash rate](/glossary/hash-rate) stops writing real timestamps. For almost every block the stamp creeps forward by one second every six blocks, the slowest pace the median rule allows, so the chain's clock falls further and further behind the wall clock. On the last block of each window the miner writes the real time, by now weeks ahead of the stamps around it. The first block of the next window drops back to the crawling clock, which the median rule permits because one outlier does not move a median of eleven. From the second window on, each period measures far longer than two weeks, so each retarget cuts difficulty: by half the first time, by more as the windows get shorter, and the miner finds blocks faster every round.

The numbers are bad: [BIP-54](/glossary/great-consensus-cleanup) puts difficulty at its minimum within 38 days of starting in the worst case, after which the attacker can mine millions of blocks, bringing forward all the remaining subsidy and breaking every time lock that Lightning channels and vaults rely on. BitMEX Research ran the same arithmetic and got more than ten blocks a second by day 39. Honest miners are what stands in the way. An honest first block in any window, stamped with the real time, spoils that round, and honest stamps elsewhere drag the median forward, so the attack needs a majority. A minority miner who bends timestamps opportunistically gains almost nothing, and Matt Corallo's 2019 draft recorded no known attempt on mainnet. [The Time Warp](/rabbit-hole/time-warp) checks that record against all 483 retargets on mainnet and runs the attack window by window in a simulation from November 2026.

The reasons it sat unfixed are mundane. The attack is slow, visible to everyone for weeks, and needs a majority that could already censor the chain, so for years it was filed under theoretical. Corallo's 2019 Great Consensus Cleanup bundled the fix with script changes that drew objections about confiscating old coins, and the whole package stalled; his pull request was closed that November. Testnet3 carries the only known time warp on a Bitcoin network: Greg Maxwell planted one in it on purpose in July 2012, so that developers could test against it, and difficulty there fell from 16 to 4 to 1 in two windows. Its later block storms were a different bug in the same code. The 20-minute minimum-difficulty exception let miners produce three years' worth of blocks in a few weeks in 2024, and BIP-94 notes that a time warp would have amplified them. Testnet4, which replaced it, enforces a time warp rule in Bitcoin Core, and on signet Bitcoin Inquisition has enforced the whole of BIP-54 since February 2026.

The fix itself is small. The first block of a difficulty window may not be stamped earlier than the last block of the previous window, minus a grace period. Corallo proposed ten minutes, because deployed mining hardware rolls the timestamp forward by up to that much while searching for a nonce, and testnet4 enforces 600 seconds through the `MAX_TIMEWARP` constant in Bitcoin Core. BIP-54 widened it to two hours as a precaution, after testnet4 exposed pool software that stamped blocks from its own clock rather than the template. Two hours matches the future-time limit, so a miner can always use the current time, and under a sustained attack it costs about three seconds a block (Pieter Wuille's simulation: 599.9997 seconds with ten minutes, 596.7211 with two hours). Bitcoin Core has refused to build a block template that breaks the rule since version 29.0, and since 32.1 one that breaks BIP-54's second timestamp rule. Mainnet enforcement is still a [soft fork](/glossary/soft-fork) away. See [Great Consensus Cleanup](/glossary/great-consensus-cleanup) for the full package and [block timestamp rules](/glossary/block-timestamp-rules) for the limits miners work within.
